Jeremiah 26–28 — On Trial, the Yoke, and the False Prophet

Study note · compiled July 31, 2026

"If a prophet prophesies about peace and the word of that prophet comes true, then it will be known that Jehovah truly sent that prophet."
Key text — Jeremiah 28:9

Focus: Three chapters about telling the truth when the truth is dangerous — and about how to tell a true prophet from a false one. In chapter 26 Jeremiah is seized and put on trial for his life for preaching against the temple. In chapter 27 he wears and sends out yoke bars, God's blunt counsel to Judah and its neighbors: submit to Babylon and live. In chapter 28 the popular prophet Hananiah publicly contradicts him, snaps the yoke, promises a quick rescue — and dies within months. The note follows the four study questions: the setting, Jehovah's view, what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped, and how it benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. These chapters again jump across the years.

  • Chapter 26 is set "in the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim" (26:1) — about 628 B.C.E., soon after Josiah's death. This is Jeremiah's "temple sermon" and the trial that followed.
  • Chapter 27 opens with the words "in the beginning of the reign of" — the text reads Jehoiakim (27:1), but the whole chapter concerns Zedekiah (envoys sent "to King Zedekiah," 27:3; God speaks "to King Zedekiah," 27:12). As the study book God's Word for Us Through Jeremiah observes, "the reference to Jehoiakim at Jeremiah 27:1 may be a transcriber's error because verses 3 and 12 refer to Zedekiah." So the chapter belongs early in Zedekiah's reign.
  • Chapter 28 is dated precisely: "In that same year, in the beginning of the reign of King Zedekiah of Judah, in the fourth year" (28:1) — about 614 B.C.E. The words "in that same year" tie chapter 27 to it, so the yoke episode and the Hananiah clash belong to the same period.

So chapter 26 (Jehoiakim, ~628 B.C.E.) stands more than a decade before chapters 27–28 (Zedekiah's fourth year, ~614 B.C.E.).

What was going on. In chapter 26, at the start of Jehoiakim's reign, Jeremiah delivers the temple sermon: unless Judah reforms, Jehovah will make the temple like the abandoned sanctuary at Shiloh. The people, who treated the temple as a good-luck charm, are enraged and seize him. The princes acquit him and Ahikam protects him — but the account also records Urijah, a prophet with the same message who was hunted down and killed, a reminder of how real the danger was. In chapters 27–28, years later, an anti-Babylon coalition is forming (envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon arrive in Jerusalem). Jeremiah wears and sends out yoke bars: God's word is to serve Babylon and live. The prophet Hananiah publicly contradicts him, breaks the yoke, and predicts the Babylonian yoke will be shattered within two years. Jeremiah foretells Hananiah's death — and it comes within months.

2.What was Jehovah's view of the situation?

Jehovah's view runs through the three chapters in several threads.

He wanted the whole message delivered, unsoftened — because there was still time to repent. "Tell them everything that I command you; do not take away a word. Perhaps they will listen and each one will turn back from his evil way, and I will change my mind concerning the calamity" (26:2, 3). The warning itself was mercy; the judgment was still conditional on their response (26:13).

He stood behind his messenger against a hostile establishment — though not with a guarantee of safety. Priests, prophets and people demanded death, yet the princes ruled, "This man does not deserve the death penalty" (26:16), and Ahikam shielded him (26:24). But God did not promise every faithful prophet a rescue in this life: Urijah, preaching the same message, was executed (26:20-23). Jeremiah's deliverance was a real mercy, not an automatic rule.

He treated the misuse of his name as a capital matter. Hananiah "caused this people to trust in a lie" and had "urged rebellion against Jehovah," so the sentence was, "This year you will die" (28:15-17). Speaking soothing falsehoods in God's name was not harmless; it drew his severest judgment.

He laid down the test of a true prophet: fulfillment. "If a prophet prophesies about peace and the word of that prophet comes true, then it will be known that Jehovah truly sent that prophet" (28:9; compare Deut. 18:20-22). Confidence, popularity and a reassuring message proved nothing — only the outcome did.

His hard counsel was aimed at preserving life. "Bring your necks under the yoke of the king of Babylon and serve him and his people, and you will keep living" (27:12). Submission to Babylon was the way to survive; it was the optimistic prophets, promising the yoke would soon break, who were "prophesying lies" that led people to death (27:14, 15).

3.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. He was delivering the most unwelcome messages imaginable to the most dangerous audiences: condemning the temple to the temple crowd (26), telling a nationalistic Judah to surrender to its enemy (27), and publicly contradicting a popular prophet to his face (28). He stood alone against priests, prophets, a king, and public opinion.

How he coped.

  • He accepted the danger without flinching, entrusting his life to God and the truth. On trial he said: "As for me, I am in your hand. Do to me whatever seems good and right in your eyes. Only know for certain that if you put me to death, you will bring innocent blood upon yourselves" (26:14, 15). He did not recant to save himself; he stated the truth calmly and left the outcome to them and to God. The study book notes that "he did not allow the opponents' menacing appearance or numbers to frighten him."
  • He kept pointing away from himself to Jehovah as his sender. "It was Jehovah who sent me to prophesy… reform your ways… and Jehovah will change his mind" (26:12, 13). His confidence rested on the commission, not on his own standing.
  • He answered a rival graciously but firmly. To Hananiah he first said, "Amen! May Jehovah do this!" (28:6) — he would have loved the good news to be true — then calmly applied the test of fulfillment (28:7-9), and pronounced God's verdict only once the word came to him (28:12-16). He did not win by shouting louder; he let God's word settle it.
  • He leaned on God rather than allies. Ahikam protected him (26:24), but he stood essentially alone. As the study book puts it, "Jeremiah… chose as his friend Jehovah rather than neighbors." God's backing, not human numbers, was his security (compare Heb. 13:6).

4.How can this account benefit me today?

  • Deliver the whole truth, even the unwelcome parts. God said, "do not take away a word" (26:2). Faithfulness means not trimming a message to make it comfortable — whether in teaching, sharing my beliefs, or giving honest counsel.
  • Doing right may set you against the majority — even against religious authority. It was the priests and prophets of the temple who wanted Jeremiah dead (26:8, 11). Popularity is no measure of truth; I should not assume the crowd, or even respected leaders, are right by default.
  • Test messages by God's word and by their fruit, not by confidence or appeal. Hananiah sounded authoritative and told people exactly what they wanted to hear — and was wrong (28:2-4, 15). The test is whether "the word of that prophet comes true" (28:9). I benefit by checking reassuring claims against Scripture and reality rather than trusting the most confident or comforting voice (compare 1 John 4:1).
  • State the truth calmly and leave the consequences to God. Jeremiah's "I am in your hand… only know that Jehovah sent me" (26:14, 15) models composure under threat: speak honestly, neither retaliate nor grovel, and entrust the outcome to God.
  • Beware telling people only what they want to hear. The false prophets promised peace and a short exile, and it cost lives (27:14-16). Flattering or falsely reassuring others "in God's name" is not kindness; truth that protects is better than comfort that misleads.
  • Choose God as your friend when you must stand alone. Jeremiah leaned on Jehovah rather than the crowd (compare Heb. 13:6). When doing right isolates me, God's presence is enough.

Key passages (New World Translation)

Jeremiah 26:2, 3

"Stand in the courtyard of the house of Jehovah and speak concerning all the people of the cities of Judah who are coming in to worship at the house of Jehovah. Tell them everything that I command you; do not take away a word. Perhaps they will listen and each one will turn back from his evil way, and I will change my mind concerning the calamity that I intend to bring on them because of their evil deeds."

Jeremiah 26:8, 9

"Then the priests and the prophets and all the people seized him and said: 'You will surely die. Why have you prophesied in the name of Jehovah, saying, "This house will become like Shiloh, and this city will be devastated and left without an inhabitant"?'"

Jeremiah 26:13

"So now, reform your ways and your actions and obey the voice of Jehovah your God, and Jehovah will change his mind concerning the calamity that he has spoken against you."

Jeremiah 26:14, 15

"But as for me, I am in your hand. Do to me whatever seems good and right in your eyes. Only know for certain that if you put me to death, you will bring innocent blood upon yourselves and upon this city and upon her inhabitants, for in truth Jehovah sent me to you to speak all these words in your hearing."

Jeremiah 26:24

"But Ahikam the son of Shaphan supported Jeremiah, so that he was not handed over to the people to be put to death."

Jeremiah 27:6

"And now I have given all these lands into the hand of my servant King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon; even the wild beasts of the field I have given him to serve him."

Jeremiah 27:12

"Bring your necks under the yoke of the king of Babylon and serve him and his people, and you will keep living."

Jeremiah 28:9

"If a prophet prophesies about peace and the word of that prophet comes true, then it will be known that Jehovah truly sent that prophet."

Jeremiah 28:11

"Hananiah then said in the presence of all the people: 'This is what Jehovah says, "Just like this I will break the yoke of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon from off the neck of all the nations within two years."' And Jeremiah the prophet went on his way."

Jeremiah 28:15-17

"Listen, please, O Hananiah! Jehovah has not sent you, but you have caused this people to trust in a lie. Therefore this is what Jehovah says, 'Look! I am removing you from the face of the ground. This year you will die, for you have urged rebellion against Jehovah.' So Hananiah the prophet died in that year, in the seventh month."
