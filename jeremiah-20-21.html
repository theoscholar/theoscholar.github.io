Jeremiah 20 & 21 — The Fire in His Bones and the Way of Life or Death

Study note · compiled July 31, 2026

"But in my heart it became like a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was tired of holding it in; I could no longer endure it."
Key text — Jeremiah 20:9

Focus: Two chapters bound together by pressure. In chapter 20 the pressure falls on Jeremiah himself — beaten and locked in the stocks by the priest Pashhur, mocked all day long, sinking into one of the rawest laments in Scripture, yet unable to quit because God's word burns in him "like a burning fire." In chapter 21 the pressure falls on Jerusalem — King Zedekiah, with Babylon at the walls, begs for a miracle, but Jehovah answers that he himself will fight against the city and sets before the people "the way of life and the way of death." The note follows the four study questions: the setting, Jehovah's view, what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped, and how it benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. These two chapters stand far apart in time, so it helps to fix each one.

  • Chapter 20 carries no date. But its place directly after the smashing of the earthenware flask at the Valley of Hinnom (ch. 19) sets it in the earlier stretch of Jeremiah's ministry, most likely during the reign of Jehoiakim (628–618 B.C.E.). Jeremiah was still years away from seeing his warnings come true, and already paying for them.
  • Chapter 21 is firmly dated to the very end. King Zedekiah sends messengers "because King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon is waging war against us" (21:2). This is the final Babylonian siege of Jerusalem, about 609–607 B.C.E., a decade or more later, under the last of Judah's kings.

So, as elsewhere in the book, an early episode is set beside a very late one.

Timeline — one early trial, one final siege (JW chronology)

647 B.C.E. — Jeremiah begins prophesying in the 13th year of Josiah (1:1-3).
c. 628–618 B.C.E. — Chapter 20 (undated) — during Jehoiakim's reign, Pashhur the priest strikes Jeremiah and puts him in the stocks; Jeremiah's raw lament and the "burning fire" in his bones (20:1-18).

What was going on. In chapter 20, Jeremiah has just publicly proclaimed doom on Jerusalem (the flask, ch. 19), and the temple's chief officer answers with violence — a beating and a night clamped in the stocks. Out of that humiliation pours a complaint against God and a curse on the day of his birth. In chapter 21, years later, the very disaster Jeremiah had foretold has arrived: Babylon is at the walls. Zedekiah, who had otherwise ignored the prophet, suddenly wants a word from Jehovah, hoping for rescue. It was too late for the nation — but not for the individual.

2.What was Jehovah's view of the situation?

Jehovah's view runs through both chapters in several threads.

He does not shield his servant from suffering, but he is present in it. Pashhur beat and stocked the prophet, and Jehovah's word to Jeremiah was not "I will spare you pain" but a promise of vindication and companionship: "Jehovah was with me like a fearsome warrior" (20:11). God permitted the mistreatment yet stood beside the man carrying it.

He renamed the persecutor by his true destiny. The priest's prestige counted for nothing. Jehovah renamed him: "not Pashhur, but Terror All Around," and spelled out his end — exile and death in Babylon "because you have prophesied lies" (20:3-6). God's assessment overrode the man's title.

He welcomed raw honesty in prayer. Jeremiah's protest — "You have fooled me, O Jehovah… and you prevailed. I have become a laughingstock" (20:7) — and even his cursing of his own birth (20:14-18) are preserved in inspired Scripture. God did not rebuke him for pouring out despair. As the study book God's Word for Us Through Jeremiah explains, God "fooled" Jeremiah only "in a positive, beneficial sense" — He "overpowered him, proving far stronger than Jeremiah and his human inclinations," so that when the prophet "thought that he had reached his limit and could not keep going," he kept going anyway.

He would not be manipulated into rescuing the unrepentant. In chapter 21 Zedekiah hoped Jehovah would "perform one of his wonderful works… so that he will withdraw from us" (21:2). The answer was the reverse: "I myself will fight against you with an outstretched hand and a mighty arm, with anger and wrath" (21:5). God would even turn the defenders' own weapons back on them (21:4). A last-minute request for a miracle, with no change of heart, would not move him.

Yet he still set a door of life before each person. Even under sentence on the city, "Here I am putting before you the way of life and the way of death" (21:8). Anyone who surrendered to the Chaldeans would "keep living" (21:9). And to the royal house he still held out the standard that could have turned back his anger: "Uphold justice every morning… so that my wrath does not blaze like a fire" (21:12). Judgment on the nation never cancelled mercy for the individual who would choose it.

3.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. In chapter 20 Jeremiah was mostly enduring — beaten, stocked, ridiculed, and then wrestling aloud with God. In chapter 21 he was the lone channel of Jehovah's word to a king in crisis, handing Zedekiah an answer no one wanted: no miracle; surrender or die. Whether the pressure came from a temple officer or from a king, he kept telling the truth when it was dangerous and unwelcome.

How he coped. Chapter 20 is one of the most candid windows into a servant's inner life anywhere in Scripture, and it shows what carried him:

  • He was honest with God about his pain. He did not fake serenity. He said he felt "fooled" and overpowered, made "a laughingstock" (20:7), and even wished he had never been born (20:14-18). The study book observes that "clearly, he knew what despair was" — and bringing those feelings to God, rather than burying them, was part of how he survived.
  • He could not contain God's word — and that itself kept him going. He resolved to quit: "I will speak no more in his name." But "in my heart it became like a burning fire shut up in my bones… I could no longer endure it" (20:9). His conviction about the message outlasted his discouragement.
  • He leaned on Jehovah as a warrior at his side. "Jehovah was with me like a fearsome warrior" (20:11). The study book renders the thought as God being "a mighty soldier standing at your side," and notes that the same God who strengthened Jeremiah "can strengthen you too" to "do far more than you ever thought possible."
  • He handed his case to God instead of avenging himself. "You, O Jehovah of armies, are examining the righteous one… Let me see your vengeance on them, for to you I have committed my legal case" (20:12). He left the judging to God.
  • He let praise and lament coexist. Directly after "Sing to Jehovah! Praise Jehovah!" (20:13) comes the curse on his birth (20:14-18). His faith did not require pretending the darkness away; he moved through both moods and kept serving — the very man "whom the Creator was able to use mightily as a spokesman."

4.How can this account benefit me today?

  • Faithfulness can cost you — and God's presence, not immunity, is the promise. Jeremiah was beaten for telling the truth (20:1, 2). Doing right does not guarantee gentle treatment, but "Jehovah was with me like a fearsome warrior" (20:11). His backing, not a trouble-free life, is what I can count on.
  • Bring your real feelings to God. Jeremiah's blunt "You have fooled me" and his despair were preserved in the Bible for a reason. I do not have to dress up my prayers or hide discouragement; God invites honesty and is not driven away by it (compare Ps. 62:8).
  • Let conviction outlast discouragement. When Jeremiah wanted to quit, the "burning fire shut up in [his] bones" would not let him (20:9). When I feel like giving up on what is right, a deep conviction about the truth can carry me past the low points.
  • Trust God to be your strength when you have hit your limit. Jeremiah did "far more than [he] ever thought possible" because God "overpowered" his weakness (on 20:7-11). When my own resources run out, I can lean on that same strength rather than on my toughness.
  • Leave vengeance to God. Instead of retaliating against Pashhur, Jeremiah "committed [his] legal case" to Jehovah (20:12). When wronged, I benefit by handing the matter to God rather than taking revenge (compare Rom. 12:19).
  • Do not wait for the crisis to seek God. Zedekiah wanted a word from Jehovah only when Babylon was already at the gate, after years of ignoring him (21:1, 2). A relationship built only in emergencies is a poor foundation; the time to listen is before the siege.
  • There is always a way of life — take it. Even in judgment, God set "the way of life and the way of death" before the people (21:8). No situation is so far gone that the path of obedience is closed. The wise course is to choose life while it is still being offered.

Key passages (New World Translation)

Jeremiah 20:1, 2

"Now Pashhur the son of Immer, the priest, who was also the leading commissioner in the house of Jehovah, was listening when Jeremiah prophesied these things. Then Pashhur struck Jeremiah the prophet and put him in the stocks that were at the Upper Gate of Benjamin, which was in the house of Jehovah."

Jeremiah 20:3, 4

"Jehovah has named you, not Pashhur, but Terror All Around. For this is what Jehovah says, 'I will make you an object of terror to yourself and to all your friends… and I will give all Judah into the hand of the king of Babylon, and he will take them into exile in Babylon and put them to death by the sword.'"

Jeremiah 20:7

"You have fooled me, O Jehovah, and I was fooled. You used your strength against me, and you prevailed. I have become a laughingstock all day long; everyone ridicules me."

Jeremiah 20:9

"So I said: 'I am not going to make mention of him, and I will speak no more in his name.' But in my heart it became like a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was tired of holding it in; I could no longer endure it."

Jeremiah 20:11

"But Jehovah was with me like a fearsome warrior. That is why those persecuting me will stumble and will not prevail."

Jeremiah 20:12, 13

"But you, O Jehovah of armies, are examining the righteous one; you see the innermost thoughts and the heart… For to you I have committed my legal case. Sing to Jehovah! Praise Jehovah! For he has rescued the poor one from the hand of evildoers."

Jeremiah 20:14, 18

"Cursed be the day I was born! May the day my mother gave birth to me not be blessed!… Why did I have to come out of the womb to see trouble and grief, to end my days in shame?"

Jeremiah 21:2

"Please inquire of Jehovah in our behalf, because King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon is waging war against us. Perhaps Jehovah will perform one of his wonderful works in our behalf, so that he will withdraw from us."

Jeremiah 21:4, 5

"Here I am turning around against you the weapons of war that are in your own hands… And I myself will fight against you with an outstretched hand and a mighty arm, with anger and wrath and great indignation."

Jeremiah 21:8, 9

"Here I am putting before you the way of life and the way of death. Those who remain in this city will die by the sword, by famine, and by pestilence. But whoever goes out and surrenders to the Chaldeans who are besieging you will keep living, and he will have his life as a spoil."

Jeremiah 21:12

"O house of David, this is what Jehovah says: 'Uphold justice every morning, and rescue the one being robbed from the hand of the defrauder, so that my wrath does not blaze like a fire and burn with no one to extinguish it because of your evil deeds.'"
