Jeremiah 32 & 33 — Buying a Field Under Siege

Study note · compiled July 31, 2026

"Here I am, Jehovah, the God of all mankind. Is there anything too wonderful for me?"
Key text — Jeremiah 32:27

Focus: With Babylon's army encircling Jerusalem and Jeremiah himself under guard for preaching surrender, God tells him to do something that looks absurd — buy a field. It is a signed, sealed, witnessed act of faith that "houses and fields and vineyards will again be bought in this land." In chapter 33 God answers Jeremiah's prayer with a sweep of restoration promises — healing, the return of joy and worship, and the "righteous sprout" of David — all sealed by the truth that nothing, not even this, is "too wonderful" for the God of all mankind. The note follows the four study questions: the setting, Jehovah's view, what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped, and how it benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. These two chapters are precisely dated and share one setting. Chapter 32 comes "in the 10th year of King Zedekiah of Judah, that is, the 18th year of Nebuchadnezzar" (32:1) — about 608 B.C.E., roughly a year before the city fell. The Babylonian army was besieging Jerusalem, and Jeremiah was "confined in the Courtyard of the Guard" for his message (32:2, 3). Chapter 33 is the word that came "the second time, while he was still confined" (33:1) — the same time and place.

Timeline — faith acted out under siege (JW chronology)

647 B.C.E. — Jeremiah begins prophesying in the 13th year of Josiah (1:1-3).
617 B.C.E. — Jeconiah is exiled to Babylon; Zedekiah becomes Judah's last king.
609 B.C.E. — Babylon's final siege of Jerusalem begins.
c. 608 B.C.E. — Chapters 32–33 — in Zedekiah's 10th year, confined in the Courtyard of the Guard, Jeremiah buys Hanamel's field and receives the restoration promises (32:1–33:26).
607 B.C.E. — Jerusalem falls and is burned, exactly as foretold (32:28, 29).
537 B.C.E. — The exiles return and fields are bought again in the land, fulfilling the sign of the purchase (32:15, 44).

What was going on. The siege was in its final stage and the outcome was certain: the city would fall and burn (32:28, 29). Yet God directed Jeremiah to buy family land — a full legal transaction with weighed silver, sealed and unsealed deeds, and witnesses — and to store the deeds in an earthenware jar "for a long time," as a physical pledge that ordinary life would resume in this land (32:14, 15). Jeremiah then prayed, wrestling aloud with the tension (32:16-25); God answered (32:26-44) and, in chapter 33, expanded the promise: healing and peace, the return of joy and worship, and an unbreakable covenant with David and the priesthood.

2.What was Jehovah's view of the situation?

Nothing is beyond his power. When Jeremiah prayed, "Nothing is too wonderful for you" (32:17), God answered by turning the words back on him: "Here I am, Jehovah, the God of all mankind. Is there anything too wonderful for me?" (32:27). The restoration of a doomed nation was not too hard for him.

He backed a hard promise with a concrete pledge. The purchased field was a guarantee in silver and sealed parchment that "houses and fields and vineyards will again be bought in this land" (32:15, 43, 44). He gave his people something tangible to hold onto.

He was honest about the judgment and its cause. The city would burn for its idolatry and stubborn disobedience — the people "turning their backs to me, not their faces," and even sacrificing their children to false gods (32:29-35). He did not pretend the coming disaster away.

But judgment was not the last word. He would regather them, give them "one heart and one way," and make "an everlasting covenant" of unfailing good: "I will exult over them to do good to them… with all my heart and with all my soul" (32:38-41).

He invites his people to ask him for insight. "Call to me, and I will answer you and readily tell you great and incomprehensible things that you have not known" (33:3). The study book notes this offer was extended "in the tenth year of King Zedekiah" — an invitation to learn from God about "the desolation of Jerusalem and its restoration after 70 years."

He guarantees the Messianic line as surely as day and night. The "righteous sprout" of David will "execute justice and righteousness," and Jerusalem will be called "Jehovah Is Our Righteousness" (33:15, 16; compare 23:5, 6). His covenant with David is as fixed as his covenant of day and night (33:20, 21, 25, 26).

3.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. In prison, with the siege closing in, he obeyed a command that must have seemed pointless — spending real silver on land about to be overrun — and then prayed his questions out honestly before God.

How he coped.

  • He obeyed even when the instruction defied common sense. He recognized God's hand behind the odd request — "At that I knew that this was by the word of Jehovah" (32:8) — and acted on faith before he understood how it could make sense.
  • He brought his perplexity to God in prayer rather than bottling it up. His prayer first rehearses God's power and faithfulness, then lays out plainly the tension he felt about buying a field in a doomed land (32:16-25). Faith and honest questions sat side by side.
  • He rested on God's power. His prayer opens, "Nothing is too wonderful for you" (32:17) — and God returned the very phrase to reassure him (32:27).
  • He took God up on the invitation to "call to me." Confined and uncertain, he received in return a flood of reassurance about the restoration to come (33:3-26).

4.How can this account benefit me today?

  • Act in faith on God's promises even when they defy the visible facts. Jeremiah bought a field in a country about to be overrun because God said restoration would come (32:15). Faith sometimes means concrete action against the odds.
  • Remember that nothing is too wonderful — too hard — for God. "Is there anything too wonderful for me?" (32:27). No situation is beyond his power to turn around.
  • Bring your honest questions to God in prayer. Jeremiah obeyed first and then prayed out his perplexity (32:16-25); trusting God and asking him hard questions are not opposites.
  • Take God up on "Call to me, and I will answer you." He invites us to seek understanding and reassurance from him (33:3).
  • Hold on to the sure hope of the "righteous sprout." Justice under the Messianic King is as certain as day and night (33:15, 20, 21).
  • God can bring good even after deserved judgment. Beyond the ruin he promised healing, joy and worship restored (33:6, 11) — a reminder that discipline is never the end of his purpose.
