Jeremiah 24 & 25 — Two Baskets of Figs and the Cup of Wrath

Study note · compiled July 31, 2026

"And I will give them a heart to know me, that I am Jehovah. They will become my people, and I will become their God, for they will return to me with all their heart."
Key text — Jeremiah 24:7

Focus: Two chapters that turn the world's judgments upside down. In a vision of two baskets of figs, Jehovah calls the exiles already carried off to Babylon the "good figs" with a future, while the people still sitting "safe" in Jerusalem are the "bad figs" marked for ruin (ch. 24). Then, in the cup of the wine of wrath, that judgment widens to Judah and to nation after nation — with the precise promise of 70 years for Babylon (ch. 25). The note follows the four study questions: the setting, Jehovah's view, what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped, and how it benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. These two chapters are not in strict date order, so it helps to fix each one.

  • Chapter 25 is dated to "the fourth year of Jehoiakim son of Josiah… which was the first year of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon" — about 624 B.C.E. By Jeremiah's own count this was his 23rd year of preaching: "from the 13th year of Josiah… to this day, these 23 years, the word of Jehovah has come to me, and I kept speaking to you again and again, but you would not listen" (25:1-3). He had already been warning Judah since 647 B.C.E. and was still being ignored.
  • Chapter 24 comes later — "after King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon had carried into exile Jeconiah son of Jehoiakim… the princes of Judah, the craftsmen, and the metalworkers" (24:1). That deportation happened in 617 B.C.E., about a decade before Jerusalem's destruction in 607 B.C.E. So the vision of the figs is spoken to the people left behind under Zedekiah, with the first wave of exiles already in Babylon.

Timeline — from Jeremiah's call to the return (JW chronology)

c. 624 B.C.E. — Chapter 25 — the 4th year of Jehoiakim, 1st year of Nebuchadnezzar. After 23 years of preaching, Jeremiah announces the 70 years and the cup of wrath (25:1-11).
617 B.C.E. — Chapter 24 — Jeconiah, the princes and the craftsmen are exiled to Babylon; the vision of the two baskets of figs is given to those left behind (24:1-10).
617–607 B.C.E. — Zedekiah reigns in Jerusalem — the "bad figs" who remain in the land (24:8).
607 B.C.E. — Jerusalem is destroyed; the 70 years of desolation begin.
537 B.C.E. — The exiles return — the 70 years end and the "good figs" are restored, just as promised (24:6; 25:12).

What was going on. Judah was in its final slide. The first deportation had already stripped away the king, the nobles and the skilled workers (24:1); a compromised Zedekiah sat on the throne; and the people who remained comforted themselves that they were the survivors, the ones God had spared. Chapter 24 shatters that assumption. Meanwhile chapter 25, spoken years earlier, had already announced the verdict on the whole region: Babylon would come, the land would "be reduced to ruins," and "these nations will have to serve the king of Babylon for 70 years" (25:11). After 23 years of warnings that went unheeded (25:3), the time for pleading was giving way to the time for judgment.

2.What was Jehovah's view of the situation?

Jehovah's view runs against every human instinct in these chapters. Four threads stand out.

He judged by the heart, not by appearances. To everyone watching, the exiles dragged off to Babylon looked cursed and the people left in Jerusalem looked blessed. Jehovah reversed the verdict. "Like these good figs, so I will regard in a good way the exiles of Judah, whom I have sent away… I will keep my eye on them for their good, and I will cause them to return to this land" (24:5, 6). The ones who "remained" — Zedekiah and the crowd who felt safe — were the inedible figs: "I will make them an object of horror… the sword, the famine, and the pestilence, until they have perished" (24:8-10). Circumstances were no measure of God's favor; the disposition of the heart was.

His discipline aimed at restoration, not destruction. Even the exile of the "good figs" was corrective, not vindictive. Jehovah's goal was a changed people: "I will give them a heart to know me… They will become my people, and I will become their God, for they will return to me with all their heart" (24:7). As the study book God's Word for Us Through Jeremiah notes on this passage, God "used Jeremiah to outline a hope for those exiled in Babylon," so that Jeremiah could tell them, "There exists a hope for your future" (compare Jer. 24:6; 31:17). The purpose of the discipline was to bring hearts back.

His judgment was certain, and it was universal. The cup of "the wine of wrath" is handed first to Judah — "starting with Jerusalem and the cities of Judah, her kings and her princes" — and then, one by one, to the surrounding nations (25:15-26). No people group is exempt: "if I am bringing calamity first on the city that bears my name, should you go unpunished? You will not go unpunished" (25:29). Jehovah "has a controversy with the nations. He will personally pass judgment on all humans" (25:31). Judgment begins with God's own house and reaches to "one end of the earth clear to the other end" (25:33).

Even his instrument of judgment was accountable. Jehovah could call the pagan Nebuchadnezzar "my servant" and use him to execute the sentence (25:9) — yet Babylon was not thereby exempt. "When 70 years have been fulfilled, I will call to account the king of Babylon and that nation for their error… and I will make the land of the Chaldeans a desolate wasteland" (25:12). Even "the king of Sheshach" drinks the cup after all the others (25:26). Being useful to God's purpose was no license; Babylon would answer for its own cruelty in turn.

3.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. In chapter 24 Jeremiah was a seer — shown a vision and asked to interpret it: "Jehovah then asked me, 'What do you see, Jeremiah?'" (24:3). In chapter 25 he was both preacher and enacted sign. He summed up a lifetime of unheeded preaching — "these 23 years… I kept speaking to you again and again, but you would not listen" (25:3) — and then took the symbolic "cup of the wine of wrath out of my hand" and "made all the nations to whom Jehovah sent me drink" (25:15-17). He was told to keep prophesying "all these words" of Jehovah's roar against the earth (25:30). It was a message of near-total judgment, and he delivered it in full.

How he coped. Announcing 23 years of rejection, national ruin, and a cup of wrath for the whole world could crush a man. What steadied Jeremiah was not stoicism but what he had been shown about Jehovah:

  • He held on to the hope hidden inside the judgment. The very same vision that condemned the "bad figs" promised the "good figs" a heart to know God and a return home (24:6, 7). Jeremiah made that hopeful view his own; the study book observes that "Jeremiah made God's view of people his own" and "could say to such ones, 'There exists a hope for your future.'" Knowing that discipline had a redemptive end let him keep speaking without despair.
  • He trusted that the judgment was in God's hands, not his. Jeremiah did not have to make the nations pay; "Jehovah has a controversy with the nations. He will personally pass judgment on all humans" (25:31). His job was to announce the verdict, not to enforce it — a load far lighter to carry.
  • He rested on the precision of God's word. The flat statement "these nations will have to serve the king of Babylon for 70 years" (25:11) is not a vague threat but a dated, measurable promise. A prophet who knew Jehovah's word would come true to the year had solid ground to stand on when the crowds mocked him.
  • He kept preaching after decades of no response. The plain fact that he could say "these 23 years" (25:3) and was still delivering God's message is itself the measure of his endurance. He did not quit when results never came; he treated faithfulness, not visible success, as the goal.

4.How can this account benefit me today?

  • Do not judge God's favor by outward circumstances. The exiles looked forsaken and the stay-behinds looked blessed, and both readings were exactly backward (24:5-8). I should be slow to conclude that hardship means God's disapproval, or that comfort and security mean his approval. Jehovah "sees what the heart is" (compare 1 Sam. 16:7); the fig that looks worst to onlookers may be the one he is preserving for a future.
  • Welcome discipline as something aimed at your good. The exile of the "good figs" was meant to give them "a heart to know me" and to bring them back "with all their heart" (24:7). Correction from God is not rejection; it is the path back to him. When I am being disciplined or refined, I benefit by asking what heart-change it is meant to produce, rather than resenting it.
  • Take the certainty of divine judgment seriously — starting with yourself. The cup went first to "the city that bears my name" (25:29). Judgment "begins with us" (compare 1 Pet. 4:17) before it reaches the nations, and no one — not even the mightiest empire — slips through. That truth is meant to move me to self-examination now, while there is still time to "turn back… from your evil ways" (25:5), not to smugness about others.
  • Keep speaking and doing what is right even when no one listens. Jeremiah preached faithfully for 23 years to people who "would not listen" (25:3) and kept going. Faithfulness, not visible results, is the standard. When my efforts to do good or to share the truth seem to bear no fruit, I can measure success the way Jeremiah did — by whether I keep delivering the message entrusted to me.
  • Trust God's timetable and leave the reckoning to him. "Seventy years" was exact, and so was the promise to "call to account the king of Babylon" when they were up (25:11, 12). I do not have to right every wrong myself or fret that evil is winning; "Jehovah has a controversy with the nations" and will settle every account on schedule (25:31). That frees me from bitterness and from taking vengeance into my own hands.
  • Being useful is not the same as being righteous. Nebuchadnezzar was God's "servant" for a task and was still judged for his own error (25:9, 12). Usefulness, position, or a role in God's purpose guarantees nothing about my standing before him. What God will "call to account" is my own conduct — so I keep my own heart, not just my usefulness, in view.

Key passages (New World Translation)

Jeremiah 24:1-3

"Then Jehovah showed me two baskets of figs set before the temple of Jehovah, after King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon had carried into exile Jeconiah son of Jehoiakim… One basket had very good figs, like early figs, but the other basket had very bad figs, so bad that they could not be eaten… 'Figs; the good figs are very good, but the bad ones are very bad, so bad that they cannot be eaten.'"

Jeremiah 24:5-7

"Like these good figs, so I will regard in a good way the exiles of Judah, whom I have sent away from this place to the land of the Chaldeans. I will keep my eye on them for their good, and I will cause them to return to this land. I will build them up, and I will not tear down; I will plant them, and I will not uproot. And I will give them a heart to know me, that I am Jehovah. They will become my people, and I will become their God, for they will return to me with all their heart."

Jeremiah 24:8-10

"So I will regard King Zedekiah of Judah, his princes, the remnant of Jerusalem who are left in this land, and those who are dwelling in the land of Egypt. I will make them an object of horror and calamity to all the kingdoms of the earth… And I will send against them the sword, the famine, and the pestilence, until they have perished from the land."

Jeremiah 25:3

"From the 13th year of Josiah son of Amon, the king of Judah, to this day, these 23 years, the word of Jehovah has come to me, and I kept speaking to you again and again, but you would not listen."

Jeremiah 25:8, 9

"Because you would not obey my words… 'I am sending for King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon, my servant,' and I will bring them against this land and against its inhabitants and against all these surrounding nations. I will devote them to destruction and make them an object of horror."

Jeremiah 25:11, 12

"And all this land will be reduced to ruins and will become an object of horror, and these nations will have to serve the king of Babylon for 70 years. But when 70 years have been fulfilled, I will call to account the king of Babylon and that nation for their error… and I will make the land of the Chaldeans a desolate wasteland for all time."

Jeremiah 25:15, 16

"Take this cup of the wine of wrath out of my hand, and make all the nations to whom I send you drink it. And they will drink and stagger and act like madmen because of the sword that I am sending among them."

Jeremiah 25:28, 29

"You must drink it! For look! if I am bringing calamity first on the city that bears my name, should you go unpunished? You will not go unpunished, for I am calling for a sword against all the inhabitants of the earth."

Jeremiah 25:30, 31

"From on high Jehovah will roar, and from his holy dwelling he will make his voice heard… A noise will resound to the ends of the earth, for Jehovah has a controversy with the nations. He will personally pass judgment on all humans."

Jeremiah 25:34

"Wail, you shepherds, and cry out! Wallow about, you majestic ones of the flock, because the time of your slaughter and your dispersion has come."
