Jeremiah 31 — The New Covenant

Study note · compiled July 31, 2026

"'I will put my law within them, and in their heart I will write it. And I will become their God, and they will become my people.'"
Key text — Jeremiah 31:33

Focus: The brightest chapter in the book of Jeremiah. Amid promises to gather and rebuild his scattered people, God unveils the crown jewel of the restoration prophecies: a new covenant — not carved on stone but written on the heart, bringing an intimate knowledge of God and, above all, the permanent forgiveness of sins. Around that centerpiece are some of the tenderest lines in Scripture: an everlasting love, a mother's grief turned to hope, a wayward son brought home, and the principle that each person answers for himself. The note follows the four study questions: the setting, Jehovah's view, what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped, and how it benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. Chapter 31 is undated, part of Jeremiah's "book of consolation" (chapters 30–33), recorded in the final years of the kingdom of Judah, about 613–607 B.C.E. Its fulfillment, however, reaches far beyond Jeremiah's day — first to the return from exile in 537 B.C.E., and ultimately to Jesus Christ, who instituted the new covenant centuries later (Luke 22:20; Heb. 8:8-12). Verse 26 suggests part of it came to Jeremiah in a restful, pleasant vision: "At this I awoke… and my sleep had been pleasurable to me."

Timeline — a promise that reaches to Christ (JW chronology)

647 B.C.E. — Jeremiah begins prophesying in the 13th year of Josiah (1:1-3).
c. 613 B.C.E. — Chapter 31 (undated) — Jeremiah records the restoration promises and the new covenant (31:1-40).
607 B.C.E. — Jerusalem is destroyed; captives are mustered at Ramah — an initial fulfillment of "Rachel weeping" (31:15).
537 B.C.E. — The exiles return — the first restoration of the scattered people (31:8-12).
33 C.E. — Jesus institutes the new covenant at the Lord's Evening Meal, the grand fulfillment of 31:31-34 (Luke 22:20; Heb. 8:8-12).

What was going on. Against the backdrop of the coming exile, God pledges to reverse it — to bring his people back "from the land of the north," turn their mourning into joy, and heal the repentant. The centerpiece (31:31-34) is a new covenant to replace the Law covenant made at Sinai and broken by the nation — a covenant that writes God's law inside people and secures lasting forgiveness. The chapter's images found layered fulfillments: "Rachel weeping" (31:15) was quoted of the slaughter of Bethlehem's infants (Matt. 2:16-18), and the new covenant itself was inaugurated by Jesus (Luke 22:20).

2.What was Jehovah's view of the situation?

His motive is everlasting, loyal love. "I have loved you with an everlasting love. That is why I have drawn you to me with loyal love" (31:3). The restoration flowed from his love, not from anything his people had earned.

He is a tender Father who still yearns over a wayward child. When Ephraim repents — "Bring me back, and I will readily turn back" — God's response is aching affection: "Is Ephraim not a precious son to me, a beloved child?… my emotions are stirred for him. And I will surely have pity on him" (31:18-20).

He holds each individual accountable. He ends the fatalism of inherited guilt: "In those days they will no longer say, 'The fathers ate sour grapes, but the teeth of the sons were set on edge.' But then each one will die for his own error" (31:29, 30). No one is doomed by an ancestor's failings.

His great new provision is inward and forgiving. "I will put my law within them, and in their heart I will write it… they will all know me, from the least to the greatest of them… For I will forgive their error, and I will no longer remember their sin" (31:33, 34). As the study book God's Word for Us Through Jeremiah explains, the Law covenant "made their sins manifest" but could not remove them; there was "a dire need for lasting forgiveness of sin," which is exactly what the new covenant provides.

His commitment is as fixed as creation itself. As long as the sun, moon and stars keep their appointed order, "the offspring of Israel" will not cease before him (31:35-37). The promise is as reliable as sunrise.

3.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. Here Jeremiah's role was to receive and record — to be the faithful bearer of comfort after chapters of doom. This is, in a sense, his reward: the prophet who had to announce disaster was entrusted with the most hopeful words of his career, and even experienced part of it as a "pleasurable" rest (31:26).

How the message sustained him.

  • It gave him a hope that outlasted the catastrophe he had to preach. "There is a reward for your activity… there is a hope for your future" (31:16, 17). Holding that future let him keep going through the present ruin.
  • It rested on a God he knew to be loyal-loving and forgiving. The everlasting love of 31:3 and the promised forgiveness of 31:34 were the character of the God he had served for decades — not wishful thinking but the settled nature of his Sender.
  • It was as certain as the heavens. A promise guaranteed by the sun, moon and stars (31:35-37) was something Jeremiah could stake everything on, whatever the mockers said.

4.How can this account benefit me today?

  • Rest in God's everlasting love. His commitment is grounded in "an everlasting love" and "loyal love" (31:3), not in my performance. That is a foundation that holds on my worst days.
  • Come back — God welcomes the repentant tenderly. He receives a returning "Ephraim" like a father over a precious child (31:18-20). No one who genuinely turns back is met with coldness.
  • Own your own choices. You are not doomed by others' failures; "each one will die for his own error" (31:29, 30). My standing before God rests on my own response to him.
  • Value the new covenant personally. Its heart is God's law written inside you, a real knowledge of him, and — decisively — the forgiveness of sins made available through Christ (31:34; Luke 22:20; Heb. 8:12).
  • Let God's standards live in your heart, not just your head. A law "written on the heart" (31:33) is internalized and loved, not merely obeyed outwardly. That is the goal to aim for.
  • Anchor hope in promises as certain as sunrise. God tied his word to the fixed order of the heavens (31:35-37); his promises are that dependable.
