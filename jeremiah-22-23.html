Jeremiah 22 & 23 — Bad Shepherds and the Righteous Sprout

Study note · compiled July 30, 2026

"He defended the legal claim of the afflicted one and the poor one, So that it went well. 'Is that not what it means to know me?' declares Jehovah."
Key text — Jeremiah 22:16

Focus: Two chapters in which Jehovah, through Jeremiah, calls Judah's last kings and its false prophets to account for abusing the flock — and, against that dark backdrop, promises a perfect King, the "righteous sprout" from David's line. The note follows the four study questions: the setting, Jehovah's view, what Jeremiah was doing and how he coped, and how it benefits us.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When. These messages belong to the final years of the kingdom of Judah, during the reigns of the sons and grandson of good King Josiah — roughly 628–617 B.C.E., about a decade or two before Jerusalem's destruction in 607 B.C.E. Jeremiah had already been prophesying since 647 B.C.E. (the 13th year of Josiah), so by now he was a seasoned prophet watching the nation slide toward ruin (Jer. 1:1-3; 25:3).

Timeline — Judah's last kings (JW chronology)

647 B.C.E. — Jeremiah begins prophesying in the 13th year of Josiah (1:1-3).
629 B.C.E. — Josiah dies — the faithful king Jehovah later holds up as the example (22:15, 16).
629 B.C.E. — Shallum (Jehoahaz) reigns three months, then is exiled to Egypt, never to return (22:10-12).
628–618 B.C.E. — Jehoiakim reigns — injustice, oppression and bloodshed; a "donkey's burial" is foretold (22:13-19).
617 B.C.E. — Coniah (Jehoiachin) reigns three months, is exiled to Babylon, and is declared "childless" as to the throne (22:24-30).
617–607 B.C.E. — Zedekiah reigns while false prophets keep promising peace (23:9-40).
607 B.C.E. — Jerusalem is destroyed.
Future — The "righteous sprout," "Jehovah Is Our Righteousness" — the promised Messiah, Jesus Christ (23:5, 6).

What was going on. After Josiah died, the nation reverted to bad kings. Each one Jeremiah names came to a bad end — one exiled to Egypt, one to a disgraceful death and a "donkey's burial," one carried off to Babylon (22:10-12, 18, 19, 24-27). The men responsible for guiding the people — kings, priests and prophets — were the very ones ruining them (23:1, 2, 11). The land was "full of adulterers," and the popular prophets soothed the people with false assurances of peace while leading them into apostasy (23:10, 14, 17). It is against this collapse of trustworthy leadership that Jehovah speaks in both chapters.

2.What was Jehovah's view of the situation?

Jehovah's view is stated plainly and forcefully. Four threads run through the two chapters.

He held the leaders accountable, not the throne's prestige. A crown gave no immunity. To the king Jehovah said, "Uphold justice and righteousness. Rescue the one being robbed… do not mistreat any foreign resident… do not shed any innocent blood" — and warned that if the royal house would not obey, "this house will become a devastated place" (22:3-5). Jehoiakim's grand cedar palace impressed Jehovah not at all: "Will you continue reigning because you outdo others in your use of cedar?" (22:15).

To Jehovah, true worship shows itself in how the powerless are treated. This is the heart of the account. Josiah "upheld justice and righteousness… He defended the legal claim of the afflicted one and the poor one… 'Is that not what it means to know me?' declares Jehovah" (22:15, 16). In God's eyes, knowing him is not a matter of ritual or royal status but of imitating his own concern for justice and for the lowly. As the book God's Word for Us Through Jeremiah puts it, Josiah "had shown extraordinary kindness and generosity toward the working class and the poor… Calling Jehoiakim's attention to Josiah's consideration for the lowly, Jehovah asked: 'Was not that a case of knowing me?'" By contrast, Jehoiakim's "eyes and heart are set only on… dishonest gain… shedding innocent blood… fraud and extortion" (22:17).

He was furious at shepherds who scattered the flock. "Woe to the shepherds who are destroying and scattering the sheep of my pasture!" (23:1). The sheep were precious to him; the neglect was personal: "You have scattered my sheep… so I will turn my attention to you because of your evil deeds" (23:2). His anger against the false prophets is just as sharp — "Both the prophet and the priest are polluted" (23:11) — and he exposes the counterfeit: "I did not send the prophets, yet they ran… But if they had stood in my inner circle, they would have made my people… turn back from their bad way" (23:21, 22). His word, he says, is "like a fire… and like a forge hammer that smashes the crag," nothing like the empty "straw" of the dreamers (23:28, 29).

Yet his view was not only judgment — it was hope. Jehovah promised to gather the remnant and "raise up over them shepherds who will really shepherd them" (23:3, 4). Then comes the summit of both chapters: "I will raise up to David a righteous sprout. And a king will reign and show insight and uphold justice and righteousness… And this is the name by which he will be called: Jehovah Is Our Righteousness" (23:5, 6). Insight on the Scriptures explains that this is "a Messianic prophecy describing the future king sprouting from David's line," and that the name "Jehovah Is Our Righteousness" shows "God's name… placed… upon his promised king… is a guarantee of [his] righteousness." That King is Jesus Christ (compare Zechariah 6:12, 13). So even while condemning bad rulers, Jehovah's deeper purpose was to point forward to the perfect one — the exact opposite of the kings Jeremiah had to rebuke.

3.What was Jeremiah doing — and how did he cope?

What he was doing. Jeremiah was carrying a message no one wanted to hear, and carrying it straight to the top. He was told, "Go down to the house of the king of Judah, and deliver this message" (22:1). He confronted reigning monarchs, named them, and foretold their disgrace — Shallum's permanent exile, Jehoiakim's "donkey's burial," Coniah's rejection (22:11-30). He publicly exposed the popular prophets as frauds (23:9-40). This was dangerous work: elsewhere in the book, "the prophet was threatened with death" for the same message (see Jer. 26:8-11). Jeremiah kept delivering it anyway. He was fulfilling a twofold commission — "to uproot and to pull down… and… to build and to plant" (Jer. 1:10) — tearing down false confidence while planting genuine hope in the coming Kingdom.

How he coped. Jeremiah was not a stoic; he felt the weight of it. His own reaction to the corruption is recorded right in this account: "Concerning the prophets: My heart is broken within me. All my bones are shaking. I am like a man who is drunk… because of Jehovah and because of his holy words" (23:9). The lies of the false prophets and the ruin of the flock genuinely grieved him. What kept him going was not personal toughness but his relationship with Jehovah and his conviction about God's word:

  • He leaned on Jehovah's own words as his strength. It was "because of Jehovah and because of his holy words" that he was so moved (23:9) — the same word he called "a fire" shut up in his bones that he could not hold back (Jer. 20:9). God's message was the source of his stamina, not his burden alone.
  • He kept the long view — the "righteous sprout." In the middle of denouncing failed kings, Jeremiah was given, and proclaimed, the promise of the perfect King (23:5, 6). Focusing on Jehovah's sure purpose let him keep speaking even when the immediate outlook was bleak.
  • He looked for the good and prayed for the people. As the Jeremiah study book notes, "While there was still hope that fellow Jews might return to Jehovah, Jeremiah prayed for them… he was looking for the good in his brothers, not thinking ill of them" (compare Jer. 18:20). His motive was to help, not to condemn.
  • He drew on Jehovah's loyal love (loving-kindness). The same account that rebukes bad kings also rests on the truth that Jehovah "does not lose patience" with those who keep praying to him and "does not tire of hearing our prayers" (compare Jer. 31:3). Jeremiah's endurance was anchored in a God he knew to be loyal.

4.How can this account benefit me today?

  • Measure "knowing God" by how you treat people — especially the lowly. The account's sharpest lesson is Jehovah's own definition: defending "the afflicted one and the poor one" is "what it means to know me" (22:16). I can ask myself whether my worship shows up in fairness, honesty and kindness toward those who can do nothing for me — the true test of whether I know Jehovah as Josiah did, rather than merely acknowledging him as Jehoiakim did (compare Jer. 9:24).
  • Position and possessions impress no one who matters. Jehoiakim's cedar-paneled, vermilion-painted palace — built on unpaid wages — counted for nothing before God (22:13-15). Chasing status or luxury at others' expense is a dead end. What "goes well" is a life of justice and generosity (22:15, 16).
  • Anyone entrusted with care of others is answerable to the Owner of the flock. The "woe" on shepherds who scatter the sheep (23:1, 2) is a standing warning to any who take the lead — in the congregation or in a family. Real shepherding means feeding, protecting and searching out the straying with humility, "not lord[ing] it over" others (1 Pet. 5:2, 3). Conversely, I can take comfort that Jehovah personally values his sheep and promises "shepherds who will really shepherd them" (23:4).
  • Test what you are told against God's own word. The people were being lulled by prophets promising "You will enjoy peace" — messages from "their own heart, not from the mouth of Jehovah" (23:16, 17). The safeguard is to prize God's word, which is "like a fire" and "a forge hammer," over comfortable "straw" (23:28, 29). I benefit by checking every teaching and every reassuring voice against the Scriptures, not by preferring whatever tells me what I want to hear.
  • Endure the way Jeremiah did — anchored in Jehovah, focused on the Kingdom. It is normal to feel heartbroken over wrongdoing (23:9); the answer is not to quit but to lean on God's word, keep the sure hope of the Messianic Kingdom in view, look for the good in others, and pray. That is how Jeremiah kept speaking under threat, and it is how I can keep doing what is right when conditions or people around me discourage me.
  • Rest your hope on the one flawless King. Every human ruler in these chapters failed. The bright center is the "righteous sprout," "Jehovah Is Our Righteousness" — Jesus Christ, who "will reign and show insight and uphold justice and righteousness in the land" (23:5, 6). The very qualities Judah's kings lacked are guaranteed in him. My confidence belongs there, not in any human government.
